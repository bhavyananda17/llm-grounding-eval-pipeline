Knowledge Distillation

Knowledge distillation is a model compression technique that transfers knowledge from a large, complex model, referred to as the teacher, to a smaller, more efficient model, referred to as the student. The fundamental insight is that the teacher model's output distribution over tokens contains rich information about the relationships between classes and features, and this information can be leveraged to train a student model that achieves performance much closer to the teacher than would be possible by training on the original labeled data alone. This technique has become essential for deploying language models in resource-constrained environments where the computational cost of running large models is prohibitive.

The standard distillation process works by training the student model to mimic the teacher model's soft probability distributions rather than the hard labels of the training data. When the teacher assigns probabilities to each possible output, these soft labels encode information about the teacher's uncertainty and the similarities between different outputs. For example, if a teacher model assigns non-zero probability to both "happy" and "joyful" as completions for a given context, the student learns that these tokens are semantically related. This soft label training provides a richer learning signal than traditional cross-entropy training against one-hot labels, where only the correct answer is rewarded.

The effectiveness of knowledge distillation depends on several factors. The temperature parameter controls the softness of the probability distributions used for distillation, with higher temperatures producing smoother distributions that reveal more about the teacher's knowledge of inter-class relationships. The capacity gap between teacher and student models also matters, as a student that is too small may lack the representational capacity to absorb the teacher's knowledge effectively. Intermediate layer distillation, where the student learns to match the teacher's internal representations rather than just the final output, has been shown to improve transfer for deeper architectural differences.

Knowledge distillation has been applied successfully across a range of language model applications. DistilBERT retains ninety-seven percent of BERT's performance while being forty percent smaller and sixty percent faster. Smaller versions of GPT and LLaMA have been created through distillation to enable deployment on consumer hardware. In the context of retrieval-augmented generation, distillation can transfer both the teacher's language understanding and its ability to effectively use retrieved context, producing compact models that maintain strong performance on knowledge-intensive tasks while requiring significantly less memory and compute.
