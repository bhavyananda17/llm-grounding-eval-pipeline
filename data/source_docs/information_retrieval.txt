Information Retrieval Foundations

Information retrieval is the science of searching for information in a collection of documents, and it forms the theoretical foundation upon which modern retrieval-augmented generation systems are built. The field emerged from the need to organize and access the growing volume of textual information, with early systems relying on Boolean queries and term frequency indexing. The vector space model, introduced in the 1970s, revolutionized the field by representing both queries and documents as vectors in a high-dimensional space, where similarity is measured by the angle between vectors rather than exact term matching.

Classical information retrieval relies on several key concepts. Term frequency measures how often a term appears in a document, while inverse document frequency downweights terms that appear across many documents, emphasizing discriminative terms that are unique to specific documents. The product of these two measures, known as TF-IDF, became the standard weighting scheme for decades and remains influential in modern systems. BM25, an extension of TF-IDF that incorporates document length normalization and saturation of term frequency, was the dominant retrieval function for many years and continues to perform competitively against neural methods on many benchmarks.

The transition from sparse to dense retrieval marked a paradigm shift in information retrieval. Sparse methods like BM25 represent documents as sparse vectors where each dimension corresponds to a term in the vocabulary, while dense methods use neural networks to compress documents and queries into low-dimensional dense vectors. Dense passage retrieval, introduced by researchers at Facebook AI, demonstrated that learning to map queries and passages into a shared embedding space could significantly outperform traditional sparse methods on open-domain question answering tasks. This dense retrieval approach enabled semantic matching between queries and documents that share meaning but use different vocabulary.

Modern information retrieval systems often combine multiple retrieval strategies in a process known as hybrid retrieval. This approach merges the lexical matching strengths of sparse methods like BM25 with the semantic understanding of dense neural retrievers, typically through reciprocal rank fusion or learned combinations. The integration of large language models into retrieval pipelines has further expanded the possibilities, with models capable of query expansion, passage re-ranking, and even generating synthetic documents to improve retrieval coverage. These advances have made information retrieval not just a standalone task but a critical component of larger AI systems that need to access and reason over external knowledge.
