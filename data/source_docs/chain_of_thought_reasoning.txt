Chain-of-Thought Reasoning

Chain-of-thought reasoning is a prompting technique that encourages language models to solve complex problems by generating intermediate reasoning steps before producing a final answer. Unlike standard prompting, where the model is expected to jump directly from a question to an answer, chain-of-thought prompting explicitly asks the model to break down a problem into logical sub-steps, similar to how a human might show their work when solving a mathematics problem. This technique was demonstrated to significantly improve performance on tasks requiring multi-step reasoning, including arithmetic, commonsense reasoning, and symbolic manipulation.

The effectiveness of chain-of-thought prompting was highlighted by research showing that large language models could match or exceed human performance on certain benchmarks when provided with examples that included intermediate reasoning steps. When a few demonstrations of step-by-step problem solving are included in the prompt, the model learns to follow a similar decomposition pattern for new problems. This in-context learning ability allows the model to adopt the reasoning strategy without any changes to its underlying weights, relying entirely on the examples provided in the prompt to guide its generation process.

Zero-shot chain-of-thought, introduced by simply appending the phrase "let us think step by step" to a question, was found to elicit reasoning behavior even without providing explicit examples. This suggests that large language models possess latent reasoning capabilities that can be activated through appropriate prompting. More advanced variants include self-consistency decoding, where multiple chain-of-thought paths are sampled and the most common final answer is selected, and tree-of-thought prompting, where the model explores multiple reasoning branches simultaneously and evaluates their promise before continuing.

Despite its strengths, chain-of-thought reasoning has notable limitations. The technique tends to work reliably only with larger models, typically those with tens of billions of parameters or more, while smaller models often produce fluent but logically flawed reasoning chains. The intermediate steps can also contain errors that compound as the chain progresses, leading to confidently stated but incorrect conclusions. Additionally, chain-of-thought prompting increases both the length of generation and the computational cost, since each reasoning step requires additional token processing. Researchers continue to explore methods to make chain-of-thought reasoning more reliable, efficient, and applicable to smaller models through techniques such as process-based reward models that evaluate individual reasoning steps rather than just the final answer.
