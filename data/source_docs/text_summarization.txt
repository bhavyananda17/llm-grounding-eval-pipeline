Text Summarization Techniques

Text summarization is the task of producing a concise and coherent representation of the important information from a longer document or set of documents. As the volume of textual information continues to grow exponentially, automated summarization has become increasingly important for applications ranging from news aggregation and scientific literature review to customer support and legal document analysis. The field encompasses two primary approaches, extractive and abstractive summarization, each with distinct strengths, limitations, and appropriate use cases.

Extractive summarization selects the most important sentences or phrases directly from the source document and combines them into a summary without generating new text. Algorithms for extractive summarization typically compute sentence importance scores based on features such as term frequency, sentence position, length, and similarity to the document centroid. More recent approaches use neural networks to learn sentence importance in an end-to-end fashion, treating summarization as a sequence labeling problem where each sentence is classified as included or excluded. Extractive methods guarantee factual consistency with the source since they reuse original text, but they often produce summaries that lack fluency, contain redundant information, and cannot compress information across multiple sentences into a single concise statement.

Abstractive summarization generates new text that captures the essence of the source document, potentially using words and phrases that do not appear in the original. Modern abstractive systems are typically encoder-decoder transformer models trained on large summarization datasets such as CNN Daily Mail and XSum. These models learn to compress and paraphrase information, producing summaries that are more fluent and concise than extractive approaches. However, abstractive models are prone to hallucination, generating plausible-sounding statements that are not supported by the source document, which is particularly problematic in domains where factual accuracy is critical.

Evaluation of summarization quality remains a challenging problem. ROUGE, which measures overlap between the generated summary and reference summaries at the n-gram level, has been the standard metric despite known limitations in capturing semantic similarity and factual accuracy. Newer evaluation approaches include BERTScore, which uses contextual embeddings to measure semantic similarity, and factual consistency metrics like FActScore that verify whether individual claims in the summary are supported by the source document. The combination of these metrics provides a more comprehensive assessment of summary quality, though no single metric fully captures all the dimensions of a good summary, including coherence, relevance, fluency, and factual accuracy.
