Beam Search and Decoding Strategies

Decoding strategies determine how a language model selects the next token to generate at each step of text production, and the choice of strategy has a significant impact on the quality, diversity, and coherence of the generated output. The simplest approach, greedy decoding, always selects the token with the highest probability at each step. While computationally efficient, greedy decoding often produces repetitive and uninspired text because it never explores alternative paths that might lead to a better overall sequence, even if individual tokens have slightly lower probabilities.

Beam search decoding addresses the limitation of greedy decoding by maintaining multiple candidate sequences, called beams, at each generation step. At every step, the algorithm expands each beam by considering all possible next tokens, keeps the top-k candidates based on their cumulative log-probability, and discards the rest. The width of the beam, denoted as k, controls the trade-off between exploration and computational cost. A beam width of one reduces beam search to greedy decoding, while wider beams explore more possibilities. Beam search has been the standard decoding method for tasks like machine translation, where finding the most likely complete sequence is more important than generating diverse outputs.

For open-ended generation tasks such as creative writing and dialogue, sampling-based methods have become more popular than beam search. Temperature scaling adjusts the sharpness of the probability distribution before sampling, with lower temperatures making the distribution more peaked and deterministic, and higher temperatures making it flatter and more random. Top-k sampling restricts the candidate tokens to the k most probable options, eliminating the long tail of unlikely tokens. Nucleus sampling, also known as top-p sampling, dynamically selects the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds a threshold p, adapting the number of candidates to the model's uncertainty at each step. These sampling methods produce more natural and diverse text than beam search for generative tasks.

Hybrid approaches combine elements of multiple strategies. Minimum length penalties prevent beam search from producing overly short sequences by penalizing beams that have not reached a target length. Repetition penalties discourage the model from repeating tokens or phrases that have already appeared in the generated text. Typical sampling restricts the candidate pool to tokens whose probability is within a certain factor of the most probable token, avoiding both the greediness of top-1 selection and the randomness of sampling from the full distribution. The choice of decoding strategy depends on the specific application, with beam search preferred for extraction-like tasks and sampling methods preferred for creative and conversational generation.
